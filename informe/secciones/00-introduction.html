Introduction

% of task time in world employment is at least partly delegable to an AI agent in 2026, and only % can be handed over in full. These two numbers are the output of the Agent Delegability Index (ADI), an open index that rates every task statement of the United States Occupational Information Network (O*NET) on how much of it a software agent can execute, maps the ratings to the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO-08), and weights them by employment in countries. This report describes how the index is built, compares it with existing measures, and reports its first finding: a delegation divide between rich and poor countries.

Two gaps motivate the index. The first is conceptual. The measures that dominate the literature on artificial intelligence and work score exposure: whether a large language model can reduce the time a worker needs for a task by at least half (Eloundou et al. 2024), or how closely the abilities an occupation requires track progress in AI applications (Felten et al. 2021; Webb 2020). Exposure was the right construct for a chatbot. It is not the right construct for an agent. Since 2025, publicly available systems run code, browse the web, operate a desktop, read and write files and work for hours on a multi-step goal (Kwa et al. 2025), and evaluations have moved from single answers to agent tasks and to deliverables drawn from real occupations (Kapoor et al. 2024; Patwardhan et al. 2025). The policy question has moved from “can the model help with this task?” to “can the worker hand this task over?” That is delegation, and the two differ in kind: a model can halve the time a radiologist spends on a report without being able to sign it, and an agent can file a tax return that no model could have sped up much, because the time was never in the drafting. Observed usage indices measure a third thing again, adoption, which depends on prices, regulation and habit as much as on capability (Anthropic 2025; Lee et al. 2026; Henseke 2026).

The second gap is geographic. Almost every task-level measure is built on O*NET and its Standard Occupational Classification (SOC), which only the United States uses for its statistics. The rest of the world reports employment in ISCO-08 (International Labour Office 2012). Studies that reach other countries do so through coarse bridges, usually at the one-digit level, or through a single national crosswalk (Gmyrek et al. 2023; Pizzinelli et al. 2023; Cazzaniga et al. 2024; Albanesi et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2023; Murugan et al. 2026; Carbonero et al. 2023). The International Labour Organization (ILO) index of generative-AI exposure is the exception that works natively in ISCO-08 (Gmyrek et al. 2025), but it measures exposure, not delegation. Nobody has measured delegability to agents in the classification that most countries use.

ADI addresses both gaps. The conceptual gap turns out to be narrower in the data than in principle: across occupations, the work that is exposed is mostly the work that is delegable (Chapter 6). The difference lies in how far delegation goes, which barrier stops it, and in which countries the work is done. The design is simple to state. Each of the 18,796 task statements in O*NET 30.2 (National Center for O*NET Development 2026) is rated by frontier language models with a fixed rubric at one of four levels: no meaningful role, augmentation, partial delegation or full delegation (Chapter 1). A blind human rating of 300 tasks drawn from a stratified validation sample of 1,500 agrees with the model consensus at α\alpha = , against a preregistered acceptance threshold of 0.60, and an agentic panel with web search agrees with it at κ\kappa = over the validation sample (Chapter 6). Tasks are weighted by importance and frequency within their O*NET occupation and carried to 433 of the 436 ISCO-08 unit groups through a documented chain of crosswalks (Annex B). Occupation scores are then weighted by employment from the ILO statistics database (ILOSTAT) to give a delegability share for each of countries with occupational employment data from 2019 onward.

We make five contributions, each meant to stand on its own.

  1. The Agent Delegability Index (ADI). An open, versioned index of AI-agent delegability for 433 ISCO-08 unit groups and countries, with three variants per occupation (share of task weight at partial delegation or above, share at full delegation, and expected share of working time delegable), bootstrap confidence intervals, and a permutation placebo. The world headline is % (partial or full delegation) and % (full delegation).

  2. A three-layer definition of delegation. One citable sentence each for augmentation, partial delegation and full delegation, written for agents with tool use rather than for chat models, with four named barriers (physical, accountability, trust, access) recorded separately from the level. Chapter 1 gives the definitions and Table 1.1 places them against eight existing measures.

  3. A task-level crosswalk and country atlas in ISCO-08. A reproducible bridge from O*NET-SOC 2019 tasks to ISCO-08 unit groups that keeps distinct task sets (an earlier matrix in the first author’s prior work collapsed the same occupations into nine), and an atlas of delegable employment for countries at the finest ISCO level each country reports, with breakdowns of Colombian employment by sex, age and formality from its household survey (exploratory).

  4. Validation and convergence. Agreement between models and across repeated calls, a blind human rating and an agentic validation panel, and preregistered convergence tests against the exposure indices of Eloundou et al.   5. Open tools for people and for agents. The full prompts, the raw model outputs, a Python package (pip install adi-index), static JSON per occupation, a llms.txt file and a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server with four tools (lookup_occupation, country_profile, top_occupations, cite), so that an AI agent that needs a delegability score can fetch it and cite it (Annex C).

The first finding we name is the delegation divide. Delegable employment is not spread evenly across the world. It rises with income: the share of employment in delegable tasks is % in high-income countries and % in middle-income countries, and a country-level regression of delegability on log GDP per capita at purchasing power parity has a slope of percentage points per log point (95% bootstrap interval ). The reason is composition. Poorer countries concentrate employment in agriculture, crafts and elementary occupations, where the binding barrier is physical, and agents have no body. We also preregistered a second edge, an adoption gap: that middle-income countries would use AI less than their delegable task base predicts. The data do not support it. Usage so far follows the delegable task base rather than lagging behind it.
