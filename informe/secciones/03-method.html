Method

Figure 3.1 summarises the pipeline: tasks are rated by models, consensus is taken across models, consensus is weighted into occupations, occupations are weighted into countries, and every step is validated or bootstrapped. The design was preregistered on the Open Science Framework on 21 October 2026, before the first call of the main pass, with an embargo until 31 January 2027 (Espinal Maya and Jiménez Londoño 2026); the hypotheses, the rater set, the rubric hash and the sample seeds are in the registration (https://osf.io/9zntf), and the exact rater configuration is in docs/MODELOS.md.

Rubric

Each task is rated with the rubric reproduced in Annex A. The prompt contains the reference technology, the four level definitions, the occupation title and the task text, and asks for a single JSON object with eight fields: level (0–3), share of the task’s working time an agent could take over (0–100), minutes a competent human needs for one instance, four barrier flags (physical, accountability, trust, access) and a confidence label. The rubric contains no example tasks, so no task in the data can anchor the rating of another. The file was frozen at registration; its SHA-256 hash is bcd076fb…0ea0e46 (full value in Annex A), and the scoring script refuses to run the main pass unless the file on disk matches it. A pilot of at most 300 tasks (seed 20261020) checked JSON compliance, cost and the call format before registration; pilot ratings are discarded (Section 3.2).

Raters: language models

Two models rate every task: Claude Sonnet 5.5 is the primary rater and Claude Haiku 5.5 the secondary rater, both by Anthropic (identifiers returned by the interface: claude-sonnet-5-5 and claude-haiku-5-5). The rater set was fixed in the registration and no model is added or dropped after seeing the main pass. Both are queried through the Claude Code command-line interface (claude -p, version 2.1.306, --output-format json) rather than through a raw API, in a neutral configuration: no tools (--tools ""), no Model Context Protocol servers (--strict-mcp-config), no session persistence (--no-session-persistence) and a fixed one-line system prompt that replaces the interface’s default, quoted here in full:

You are an expert rater of occupational tasks. Follow the user instructions exactly.

Each call carries one task, with no other task or rating in context. Every call is logged with the interface version, the model identifier returned by the interface, the prompt hash, the duration and the outcome in docs/llm-llamadas.jsonl, which doubles as the manuscript’s declaration of AI use; the configuration is recorded in docs/MODELOS.md.

The interface does not expose a temperature setting, so the ratings are not deterministic by construction. We therefore measure stability directly: a random subsample of tasks is rated a second time by each model under identical prompts, and we report exact agreement and quadratic-weighted kappa between the two passes (Sonnet: ; Haiku: ). Calls whose output fails validation (malformed JSON, a level outside 0–3, a share outside 0–100) are retried up to five times; tasks that still fail are excluded for that model only, and the counts are reported ( for Sonnet, for Haiku).

The call format was settled in the discarded pilot by two tests, both run before registration. First, rating tasks in batches of per call agreed with one-task calls at quadratic-weighted κ\kappa = for Sonnet and for Haiku, below the preregistered floor of 0.80, so batching was rejected and the main pass uses one task per call. Second, the interface’s default wrapper, which tells the model that it is a coding agent with tools, raised the mean level from under the neutral configuration to (agreement between the two, κ\kappa = ). The main pass therefore uses the neutral configuration, and a preregistered sensitivity analysis re-rates the 1,500 tasks of the validation sample with the default wrapper (Section 3.4 and 6).

Aggregation

Let tt index tasks, mm models, oo ISCO-08 unit groups and cc countries. The consensus level of a task is the level both models give when they agree and the lower of the two levels when they do not (the preregistered modal rule with ties resolved downward, applied to two raters); the consensus share is the median, here the mean, of the two models’ shares. Task weights wotw_{ot} are importance times mean frequency, normalised within the O*NET occupation, multiplied by the crosswalk weight (1/k1/k for a SOC code that maps to kk ISCO groups) and renormalised so that ∑twot=1\sum_t w_{ot} = 1 within each unit group. The primary occupation index is the share of task weight at partial delegation or above: ADIo=∑twot𝟏[levelt≥2],\begin{equation} \mathrm{ADI}_o = \sum_{t} w_{ot}\, \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{level}_t \geq 2], \label{eq:adi_o} \end{equation} with two preregistered variants, the share at full delegation and the expected share of working time delegable: ADI3o=∑twot𝟏[levelt=3],ADIso=∑twotsharet100.\begin{equation} \mathrm{ADI3}_o = \sum_{t} w_{ot}\, \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{level}_t = 3], \qquad \mathrm{ADIs}_o = \sum_{t} w_{ot}\, \frac{\mathrm{share}_t}{100}. \label{eq:adi_var} \end{equation} The country index is the employment-weighted mean over the occupational groups gg the country reports: ADIc=∑gecgADIg∑gecg,\begin{equation} \mathrm{ADI}_c = \frac{\sum_{g} e_{cg}\, \mathrm{ADI}_g}{\sum_{g} e_{cg}}, \label{eq:adi_c} \end{equation} where ecge_{cg} is ILOSTAT employment and gg runs over two-digit sub-major groups if the country reports them for a year from 2019 onward and over one-digit major groups otherwise. Within a reported group, unit-group indices are averaged with employment weights from microdata of a country in the same World Bank income group, as preregistered. No country publishes employment at the four-digit level in ILOSTAT, so we borrow the within-group structure from two sources mapped through the same bridge as the tasks: United States Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics for May 2024 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2025) for high-income countries, and Colombia’s household survey (GEIH, 2024–2026) (Departamento Administrativo Nacional de Estadística (DANE) 2026) for middle- and low-income countries. The second is a stated loan for lower-middle and low-income countries, where no four-digit microdata were available to us. Two sensitivity checks are reported: equal weights within each group (preregistered) and GEIH weights for every country. The world headline is the employment-weighted mean of ADIc\mathrm{ADI}_c over the included countries, and the income-group figures are the same mean within each group.

Human and agentic validation

The registration specifies three human raters who each rate the same 1,500 tasks blind (Espinal Maya and Jiménez Londoño 2026). Before any result of the main pass was opened, we replaced that design for reasons of time and cost; the change is dated and justified as deviation D2 in docs/DESVIACIONES.md. The validation now has two parts.

Blind human rating. A subsample of 300 of the 1,500 validation tasks, stratified by ISCO-08 major group with at least 20 tasks per group (seed 20261025; validacion/santiago/muestra_300.csv), was rated blind by a group led by Santiago Jiménez Londoño, a coauthor, with the help of student raters. The 1,500-task sample is the registered one, stratified by major group in proportion to world employment (validacion/muestra.py, seed 20261020). The raters worked from the frozen rubric and the rater guide, were trained on 20 practice tasks outside the sample, rated in a spreadsheet that contained no model or panel output and no result of the study, and did not use AI tools while rating. Which rater scored which task was not recorded, so the sheet is analysed as one human rating. We report Krippendorff’s alpha for ordinal data (Krippendorff 2004) and quadratic-weighted kappa (Cohen 1968) between that rating and the model consensus (α\alpha = ; κ\kappa = ; exact agreement %; tasks), and kappa with each model separately (Sonnet ; Haiku ). The preregistered acceptance threshold for the primary rater, α≥0.60\alpha \geq 0.60, is kept and results are reported whatever the value. Two features of this design are limitations, not details. The rating was led by an author of the study, not by independent raters in the sense of the registration; and because individual contributions were not recorded, no human–human agreement can be computed, so the human figures carry no estimate of how much humans disagree among themselves. With 300 instead of 1,500 tasks the confidence interval of alpha is also wider than the registered design allowed for.

An agentic panel, reported as non-human validation. Claude Opus 5.5, a model that plays no part in the index, rates the 1,500 validation tasks through the same command-line interface (claude -p) with web search enabled, at most four searches per task; it applies the same rubric and returns up to three pieces of cited evidence with each rating (validacion/agentico/panel.py). The panel tests whether a rater that can look up how a task is actually done, and what rules govern it, reaches the levels that the raters without tools assign. We report its agreement with the model consensus, with each model and, on the tasks they share, with the human rating. The panel is a language model from the same developer as the raters, so it is not human validation and is never reported as such.

The registered design, three human raters on all 1,500 tasks, is left for a second version of the study.

Sensitivity to the interface wrapper. As preregistered, each model re-rates the 1,500 validation tasks under the interface’s default system prompt, which describes the model as a coding agent with tools, instead of the neutral configuration (scoring/salidas/envoltorio_cc/). We report, model by model and on the same tasks, the mean level and the share at L2 or above under each configuration, quadratic-weighted kappa between the two, and the agreement of each with the human rating.

Convergent validity

Hypothesis H3 states that occupation-level ADI correlates positively with the exposure scores of Eloundou et al. (2024) and the AIOE of Felten et al. (2021), with Pearson rr between 0.40 and 0.80 for each: above 0.80 would mean ADI adds little beyond exposure, below 0.40 would question its validity. Both comparison indices are carried to ISCO-08 with the bridge of Chapter 2. Hypothesis tests

H1, the delegation divide, is an ordinary least squares regression of ADIc\mathrm{ADI}_c on log GDP per capita at purchasing power parity, and a second specification with income-group dummies with high income as reference; the test on the slope is one-sided at α\alpha = 0.05. H2, the adoption gap, standardises ADIc\mathrm{ADI}_c and AEI usage per working-age adult to mean zero and unit variance over the countries with both and regresses the difference on income-group dummies; the one-sided test is that the middle-income coefficients are positive. H4, task length, is a logistic regression of full delegation on log human minutes and its square at the task level, with standard errors clustered by occupation and minutes winsorised at 1 and 4,800; the hypothesis is a positive linear and a negative quadratic coefficient.

Inference and placebo

Uncertainty from the rating step is propagated by a nonparametric bootstrap over tasks: 1,000 draws, tasks resampled within occupation, each draw recomputing Equations (1)–(3), the headline and the regression coefficients (Efron and Tibshirani 1993). All intervals in this report are 95% percentile intervals from this bootstrap unless stated otherwise. As a placebo, we shuffle task ratings across occupations 1,000 times, holding each occupation’s task count and weights fixed, and recompute the occupational index; the observed dispersion of ADIo\mathrm{ADI}_o across unit groups is compared with this null (). A rating that carried no occupation-specific information would produce occupational indices indistinguishable from the shuffled ones. Robustness checks preregistered as exploratory are reported model by model, with ESCO skills instead of O*NET tasks, with uniform task weights, and without the seven imputed groups.
