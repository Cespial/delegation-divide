Limitations and ethics

Technical delegability is not deployment

ADI measures what an agent can do with the technology of October 2026, given the goal and access. It does not measure what firms do, what regulators allow or what workers accept. A task at L3 may never be delegated because the agent is too expensive relative to local wages, because the systems it would need are not connected, or because nobody is willing to be accountable for its output. The index is therefore an upper bound on delegation, and the comparison with observed usage in Chapter 6 is a comparison of a ceiling with a floor. Adoption of generative AI has been fast among workers (Bick et al. 2024), but its measured effects on earnings and hours have so far been small (Humlum and Vestergaard 2025), with early employment losses concentrated among young workers in exposed occupations (Brynjolfsson et al. 2025). Readers who need a forecast of employment effects should combine ADI with adoption, prices and institutions, as Acemoglu (2025) does for exposure; the index alone supports none. The reference technology is also a moving target. We fix it by date and version the index (v2026.1) so that later releases can be compared with this one rather than silently replacing it.

O*NET describes work in the United States

The task statements, and the importance and frequency weights, come from surveys of United States workers. The same occupation elsewhere may contain different tasks, in different proportions, with different tools. The crosswalk to ISCO-08 carries the United States task mix to every country; it does not adapt it. The problem is sharpest in agriculture. O*NET has only task statements whose main ISCO group lies in major group 6, skilled agricultural, forestry and fishery workers, which employs a large share of workers in low-income countries, and of them fall in the human-validation sample. Since the delegation divide (H1) depends on that group, we rerun the country regression with the ESCO skill bank in place of O*NET tasks for major group 6 and for all groups; the slope is with ESCO for all groups and with ESCO for major group 6 only, against with O*NET. The same transfer problem is known from the measurement of teleworkability: a task-based feasibility measure built on O*NET (Dingel and Neiman 2020) overstates what can be done at home in poorer countries, where the same occupations are performed with fewer digital tools (Gottlieb et al. 2021), and the access barrier in ADI plays the role that connectivity plays there (Gmyrek et al. 2024). Informal and subsistence work, which household surveys capture better than labour-force statistics by occupation, is treated in the microdata breakdowns rather than in the headline.

Language models as raters

The raters are two language models from a single developer, and the usage data in Chapter 6 come from the same developer’s Anthropic Economic Index. Three risks follow. The first is bias: a model may rate tasks in its own domain, such as software, as more delegable than tasks it knows less well, and may share the blind spots of its training data about work outside rich countries. The human check that would bound this bias is weaker than the one we registered (deviation D2). The human rating of 300 tasks was led by a coauthor with the help of student raters: they were blind to the model ratings and to the results, but the lead shares the authors’ framing of the question, and because which rater scored which task was not recorded there is no measure of how much humans disagree with each other. Agreement with that rating bounds the bias only as far as a few informed readers’ judgement does, and neither they nor any small panel of graduates is a sample of the world’s workers. The agentic panel does not fill that gap: Claude Opus 5.5 with web search brings evidence about how tasks are done, but it comes from the same developer as the two raters and may share their view of which work suits a model, so its agreement with them is a check of consistency, not of validity. Three independent human raters on the full 1,500 tasks remain the registered standard and are left for a second version. The second is circularity: if the same family of models both rates delegability and generates the usage data, agreement between the two could reflect a shared view of the world rather than validation. We address it by testing convergence against indices built by other methods and other raters (Eloundou et al. 2024; Felten et al. 2021; Gmyrek et al. 2025), by comparing with the Agentic Adoption Index, which uses no model rating (Lee et al. 2026), and by reporting the Colombian wage dynamics, which involve no model at all. The third is instability: the command-line interface exposes no temperature setting, so the same prompt can return different levels on different days. The test–retest statistics in Chapter 3 quantify this, and the consensus rule across models dampens it; the raw outputs of every pass are released so that others can recompute. A fourth risk is framing. In the pilot, a model told that it is an agent rated tasks as more delegable: the interface’s default wrapper raised the mean level from to on the same tasks. The ratings therefore depend on how the rater is addressed, not only on the task. We fix the neutral configuration for the main pass and report, as a preregistered secondary result, the same validation tasks rated under the default wrapper (Chapter 6), so that readers can see how much of the index moves with the frame. Model-based rating of social facts has a short literature (Horton 2023; Argyle et al. 2023; Korinek 2023), including evidence that model-simulated survey respondents miss the results of human polls (Chapekis et al. 2026), and the known tendency of models to favour their own outputs (Zheng et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024) is a reason to read every number here as a model’s judgement, not a measurement of the world.

Preregistration

The hypotheses, rubric, rater set, call configuration, sample seeds and analysis plan were registered on the Open Science Framework on 21 October 2026, before the main pass, under embargo until 31 January 2027 (Espinal Maya and Jiménez Londoño 2026). The registration constrains what we can report as confirmatory; everything else is labelled exploratory. Deviations from the registered plan, such as the source of the employment weights for unit groups within a reported group, are dated in docs/DESVIACIONES.md. A preregistration does not make a measurement right; it makes it checkable.

Ethics

An index of delegability describes work in the voice of the machine: which parts of a job it could take, and which it could not. That framing has costs. It can reduce a worker to the residue of tasks an agent cannot do, and it can read a barrier such as trust or care as a temporary obstacle rather than as the point of the job. Whether agents complement or replace the people who do these tasks is a design choice as much as a technical fact (Autor 2024), and a world with less work is not self-evidently a worse one (Danaher 2019). We have tried to keep the four barriers visible rather than fold them into a single score, so that a reader can see why a task stays with a human and not only that it does. The divide we name is also not symmetrical in its meaning. In high-income countries, high delegability describes a labour market about to reorganise; in low-income countries, low delegability describes a labour market whose work agents cannot reach, which is not the same as protection. Who is delegated, who delegates, and who is left outside the scope of delegation are distributional questions, and the atlas is meant to put them on the table, not to settle them. We use no personal data; all inputs are public statistics, public classifications or anonymised household surveys used under their licences. The human rating in this version was led by a coauthor as part of his work on the paper, with the help of student raters; no external professional raters were recruited, the student raters are not identified, and no personal data about raters are collected or released.
