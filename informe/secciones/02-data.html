Data

Tasks: O*NET 30.2

The unit of rating is the task statement of the Occupational Information Network (O*NET), release 30.2 (National Center for O*NET Development 2026). The release contains task statements for O*NET-SOC 2019 occupations, each with its occupation title, and two ratings per task from incumbent surveys: importance (scale 1–5) and frequency (seven categories from yearly or less to hourly or more). We use the mean importance and the mean frequency category of each task as its weight within its occupation, following the task-weighting convention of Acemoglu and Autor (2011). Missing importance or frequency values are imputed with the median, as preregistered. Each task is rated once; a task that appears under several occupations keeps one rating and several weights.

Bridge to ISCO-08

Most countries report employment in the International Standard Classification of Occupations, ISCO-08 (International Labour Office 2012), which has 436 unit groups at four digits. O*NET uses the United States Standard Occupational Classification (SOC). We bridge the two in three documented steps: O*NET-SOC 2019 to O*NET-SOC 2010 with the official O*NET Center crosswalk; O*NET-SOC 2010 to six-digit SOC 2010 by truncation; and SOC 2010 to ISCO-08 with the crosswalk of Hardy et al. (2018), published by the Institute for Structural Research (IBS). A SOC code that maps to kk ISCO groups splits its weight 1/k1/k. The result covers of the 436 unit groups with distinct task sets; the three military groups (0110, 0210, 0310) have no O*NET tasks and are excluded, and seven residual groups without tasks of their own inherit the tasks of their three-digit parent and are flagged as imputed. Four O*NET-SOC 2019 occupations with no 2010 ancestor are assigned by hand. Annex B lists every step, the two SOC codes rescued because the IBS file omits them, the seven imputed groups and the four manual assignments; the code is datos/construir_crosswalk.py.

The bridge replaces the occupation–task matrix used in the first author’s earlier work on augmented human capital (Espinal Maya 2026a, 2026b), which mapped SOC to ISCO at one digit and so collapsed Colombian occupations into task sets. Chapter 7 returns to the consequences.

Secondary task bank: ESCO

O*NET describes work in the United States. As a check on that origin, we also score the essential skills and competences that the European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations classification (ESCO, version 1.2.1) attaches to each of its occupations (European Commission 2026). ESCO maps every occupation to an ISCO-08 unit group by construction, so it needs no crosswalk, and it is available in 28 languages. Its units are skills, not tasks, so we treat it as a robustness bank rather than as the primary source. We keep only items of the skill and competence type and drop knowledge items, because the rubric rates activities that can be handed over and a body of knowledge is not one. The bank has distinct essential skills and competences in unit groups; each is rated once, by Sonnet alone, with the same rubric and the occupation for which it is most often essential, and enters each unit group with equal weight. The ESCO results are reported in Chapter 5 as a sensitivity check and matter most for agriculture, where O*NET has few tasks (Chapter 7).

Employment by occupation: ILOSTAT

Country weights come from the ILO statistics database, ILOSTAT, series on employment by sex and occupation (International Labour Organization 2026), downloaded through its SDMX interface. At one digit, countries report employment by ISCO-08 major group and have a reference year of 2019 or later; one of them (Bhutan) reports only armed-forces and unclassified employment in its latest year, so countries form the atlas. At two digits, countries report sub-major groups and have a year of 2019 or later. We use the finest level each country reports, two digits where the sub-major groups are complete (at least thirty groups whose total is within five per cent of the one-digit total) and one digit otherwise, with the pooling rule of Chapter 3: countries enter at two digits and at one. Countries whose latest occupational data predate 2019 are excluded, as preregistered. Table 2.1, generated by analisis/tablas.py, lists coverage by income group. The same database supplies the informality rate and mean monthly earnings by occupation where they exist, used in the atlas.

Income and adoption

Income groups follow the World Bank classification for fiscal year 2026, and GDP per capita is at purchasing power parity in constant 2021 international dollars (World Bank 2026). Observed adoption comes from the Anthropic Economic Index (AEI), which reports usage of Claude by country per working-age adult in its latest release (Anthropic 2025). For convergence tests we use the occupation-level scores of Eloundou et al. (2024), Felten et al. (2021), Webb (2020), Eisfeldt et al. (2026) and Gmyrek et al. (2025), each carried to ISCO-08 with the same bridge where it is not already in ISCO-08.

Household microdata

For breakdowns by sex, age and informality we use four household surveys that code occupation at four digits in national adaptations of ISCO-08: the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares of Colombia (GEIH) (Departamento Administrativo Nacional de Estadística (DANE) 2026), the Pesquisa Nacional por Amostra de Domicílios Contínua of Brazil (PNADC) (Instituto Brasileiro de Geografia e Estatística (IBGE) 2026), the Encuesta Nacional de Ocupación y Empleo of Mexico (ENOE) (Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (INEGI) 2026) and the Encuesta Nacional de Hogares of Peru (ENAHO) (Instituto Nacional de Estadística e Informática (INEI) 2026). For Colombia we also use the 2023–2026 monthly series to relate delegability to wage dynamics (Chapter 6). Every input file is listed with its SHA-256 hash and download date in datos/MANIFIESTO.jsonl.
