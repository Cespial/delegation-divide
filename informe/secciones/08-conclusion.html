Conclusion

% of task time in world employment is at least partly delegable to AI agents, and only % can be handed over in full. Agents, as the models judge them, take over parts of tasks far more often than whole tasks, and whole tasks mostly when they take a human about twelve minutes.

The share varies with income, from % in low-income to % in high-income countries. This delegation divide is a matter of composition: poorer countries employ more people in work with a physical barrier, and agents have no body. It is not evidence that the same task is less delegable in a poorer country, and it is not protection; it describes where agents can and cannot reach.

Two preregistered expectations failed, and both failures are informative. The adoption gap did not appear: observed AI usage tracks delegability closely across countries (rr = ), and middle-income countries do not lag behind their delegable task base. And at the occupation level ADI correlates with exposure indices above the bound we set in advance ( with Eloundou et al., with Felten et al.). The occupations an agent could take over are, for the most part, the ones a language model could already speed up. ADI’s contribution is not a new ranking of occupations. It is delegation levels and barriers on top of exposure, for the world: a scale that separates partial from full delegation, a record of why each task stops short, and a task-level index in ISCO-08 for countries, which correlates far less with observed usage than with exposure.
