\cifra{titular.global.l2}\% of task time in world employment is delegable to AI agents in 2026, and \cifra{titular.global.l3}\% is fully delegable.
Existing measures score the \emph{exposure} of occupations to generative AI: whether a language model can cut the time a task takes.
They do not measure \emph{delegability}: whether an AI agent with tool use and computer use can carry a task out end to end.
They are also built on the United States O*NET classification, which most countries do not use.
We build the Agent Delegability Index (ADI).
We rate all 18,796 O*NET 30.2 task statements on a frozen three-level rubric (augmentation, partial delegation, full delegation) with frontier language models, validate the ratings against three independent human raters on 1,500 tasks (Krippendorff's $\alpha$ = \cifra{agree.alpha.humano.modelo}), map tasks to 433 ISCO-08 unit groups, and aggregate to \cifra{diseno.atlas.paises} countries with ILOSTAT employment by occupation.
Three findings.
First, delegable employment rises with income: \cifra{titular.ingreso.medio.l2}\% in middle-income countries against \cifra{titular.ingreso.alto.l2}\% in high-income countries, a slope of \cifra{h1.pendiente} percentage points per log point of GDP per capita.
We call this the \emph{delegation divide}.
Second, among countries with observed AI usage, the standardized gap between technical delegability and usage is \cifra{h2.brecha.medio} standard deviations wider in middle-income countries than in high-income countries.
Third, ADI converges with exposure indices ($r$ = \cifra{h3.r.eloundou} with Eloundou et al.; \cifra{h3.r.felten} with Felten et al.) but reorders occupations where physical presence, accountability or system access bar an agent.
We release the index, the country atlas, the prompts and an MCP server at \url{https://github.com/Cespial/delegation-divide}.
